While recovering from surgery, I found myself wondering whether the people checking on me would have asked if I had stayed in Balakhu. It was an unfair question, and I didn’t enjoy having it. But it kept leading me back to the people who helped me when I had very little to offer in return. I haven’t forgotten them, or what that kind of attention was worth.

For those who haven’t heard: on Monday, October 5, I had surgery on my lower back. It went well, and I am home recovering.

The trouble goes back to the Navy. I had just reported to my first ship, and it was my first time underway. My rack was the top one in a stack of three. One night, with the Pacific rough and the ship rolling, I felt a little crack in my lower back. I heard it, too. I had no way of knowing how many years I would spend thinking about that night.

The pain began there and stayed. I tried what people usually try: injections, physical therapy, massage, chiropractic care. Each was worth trying. None of them solved it. An MRI eventually showed a herniated disc at L5-S1, between the lowest vertebra of the lower back and the sacrum.

On bad days I couldn’t sit, stand, or walk for long, and the choice among them came down to which would hurt least for the next hour. I have worked from home for years. That sounds like the right arrangement for a bad back until you notice how much of the work is done sitting down.

I put off surgery for a long time. Eventually I ran out of reasons to wait. The operation was Monday afternoon at three, and I spent one night in the hospital.

Recovery involves a great deal of lying still, and while I lay there, my mind kept going back to Balakhu.

We went outside to get warm.

The sun could be bright over Balakhu while our classroom was still cold. Its walls were mud and stone, its roof corrugated metal, jasta pata. On some winter mornings, we took our lessons into the schoolyard and sat on the ground. Beyond the yard, narrow terraces climbed the steep green hills. Houses were scattered along the slopes, and higher up, the hills rose above the school roof until they filled much of the view. We studied there in the sunshine.

This was the late 1990s, in the hills of Okhaldhunga in eastern Nepal. I knew those slopes as places where people farmed and lived, where a path led to someone’s house. There was no road to our village then. If you needed to see someone, you walked.

I didn’t know what a classroom in America looked like. I wasn’t comparing our school with one in Kathmandu. The other children were my friends; the teachers were the teachers we had. The hills around Balakhu were familiar in the way that streets and intersections would become familiar to me much later. They were where people lived, where we went to school, where you walked when there was somewhere you needed to be.

There was no road to the village then. No electricity. I began learning English in fourth grade and saw a telephone for the first time in sixth. I didn’t use it. I remember seeing it.

When someone was ill, you went to their house. News came through another person, and people left their work to visit. I remember the walking, the sitting with the family, the questions about how the sick person was feeling. There were animals to feed and fields to attend to, children and meals and unfinished work. The visit had to fit into a day already full of things that would not do themselves. Sometimes it took a good part of the afternoon.

As a child, I paid very little attention to that. I understood that somebody was sick and that people were going over. Only now do I find myself thinking about what they had put down before leaving, and what they would have to return to afterward. They made time without having much time to spare.

I would be wary of my own memory if it offered me a village of uniformly good people. Balakhu had gossip, disagreements, families who didn’t get along. The visitors were people I knew, with failings I knew something about and others I was too young to notice. But they came. Whatever had happened between them on other days, on that day they sat with someone who was unwell.

This week, I have been remembering those visits from my home in America, where I am recovering from surgery and have made myself considerably harder to reach.

The surgery went well, and the early improvement in my leg pain has been a relief I am still getting used to. I also deactivated Instagram and WhatsApp.

I didn’t want to talk.

I knew people would ask what had happened, how the operation went, whether I was home, whether I was feeling better. Most would ask because they cared. I appreciated that, but I was tired of having a back that required so much of my attention and didn’t feel like making it the subject of several simultaneous conversations. Someone could mean me nothing but kindness and still reach me at a moment when I had no wish to answer.

It is awkward to admit this while remembering, with such affection, people who walked across a village to visit the sick. I know how it sounds. The person who admires those visits has switched off the modern equivalents and is hoping everyone will understand.

I haven’t found a perfectly agreeable explanation. I have been in pain for a long time. I am less sociable than I used to be. I enjoy my own company, sometimes too much. And I have become uneasy about certain kinds of attention that arrived only after my life began to look successful.

If I were still in Balakhu, I sometimes wonder, would you be asking how I am?

I don’t mean you would be obliged to travel there. I mean whether the interest would survive a less impressive answer to the question of what I do. Whether you would want to know about the illness if you knew nothing about the employer or the university. There are people for whom the answer is plainly yes. There are others about whom I am less sure.

For a while I tried to leave that out of this essay. It seemed unfair to people who had sent a kind message, and ungracious in someone with so much to be thankful for. But it is part of what I have been thinking, and I would rather say it carefully than offer a cheerful account of recovery that avoids everything making me uncomfortable.

Some of us haven’t spoken in years. Here, then, is a longer answer to how I’ve been.

Around 2000, when I was about twelve, my mother took me with her to a training program she had to attend for work. We walked for an entire day before we could reach a bus. I had never seen one before.

We spent the night at what people called a hotel. It was a house where travelers could sleep, and it served that purpose. I occasionally wonder how often the blankets and bedsheets were washed. I didn’t inquire at the time. There are probably happier questions to bring to a childhood memory.

The next morning I boarded my first bus. I remember the smell of diesel and the view through the window. There were vehicles, shops, people moving among them, vendors carrying pots of cucumbers on their heads. Everything I saw invited another look. People who lived there could afford to be bored by it. I couldn’t imagine having seen enough of any of it to stop watching.

Even now, diesel can bring back something of that excitement. The smell belongs, for me, to those early departures from the village. I realize it is an odd thing to associate with pleasure. But before I knew much about vehicles, fuel, or cities, I knew that smell meant I was on my way to a place I had never been.

At one stop I had to cross in front of the bus. I was frightened that it would hit me and couldn’t make myself step out.

It was parked.

Other people walked past it without hesitation. They understood something about this large object that I didn’t. The fact that it wasn’t moving at that moment did very little to reassure me about what it might do at the next one. I stood there, wanting to cross, afraid of the bus and embarrassed that crossing seemed to require so much courage from me and so little from everyone else.

That embarrassment is familiar to anyone who has arrived somewhere late in the education everyone else appears to have received. You aren’t necessarily incapable of doing what they do. You may simply be encountering it for the first time, in public, where the instruction comes with an audience.

A few years later, after completing my School Leaving Certificate, a relative in the city offered me ice cream. I ate it and threw the cone away. As far as I could tell, the cone was something provided to hold the ice cream. I had finished the food. What further use could I have for its container?

My relatives laughed. I hadn’t known that you could eat that part.

This happened in the early 2000s. Elsewhere, children were growing up with computers, mobile phones, video games. I was learning something about ice cream that would scarcely have occurred to them as something one had to learn. I find it funny now. At the time I wished someone had told me before I threw it away.

I have thought about that small embarrassment more often as a teacher than I ever expected to. So much of what looks like quickness in a classroom depends on what a student encountered before entering it. A word is familiar. A process resembles something they have done. They can spend their attention on the difficult part because the ordinary parts have already been explained. Someone beside them may be learning all of it at once.

I am not always patient enough to remember this while teaching. Once something becomes obvious to you, it takes effort to imagine not knowing it. But I have a rather useful memory of standing with an ice cream and getting an elementary part of the procedure wrong. Nothing about my ability to understand a cone had changed when my relatives explained it. I had simply acquired a fact they already possessed.

For a long time, I gave people from the city credit for considerably more than the facts they possessed. I thought they understood life. They spoke English easily, attended expensive schools, had parents who knew about universities and careers. They seemed at home in places where I was still watching other people to work out what to do.

Their advantages were real. A parent who can explain an application saves a child more than the time spent filling it out. The child is also spared days of wondering whether they are even allowed to apply. I would never dismiss that difference now, having spent so much time trying to find things out alone.

But I made a second assumption. I thought their ease meant they were fundamentally different from me. I mistook being accustomed to a place for being qualified to inhabit it. If they moved confidently through a city and I was frightened of a stationary bus, surely the difference extended well beyond buses.

Years later, working and studying among people I had once imagined from a distance, I began to see how much I had added to what I actually knew about them. Before that, though, I had to get far enough to meet them.

Around 2003, a friend in Kathmandu asked whether I had an email address. I asked what an email address was. He took me to a cybercafé, we paid for computer time, and he helped me set up an account. I remember looking at it afterward, uncertain what was supposed to happen next. I had nobody in particular to write to. There was no reason to expect a message.

What interested me was the possibility of it. I wanted to understand how people could send things to one another this way. I learned about computers and the internet, then began writing about the things I was learning. Some of my technology articles in Nepal were written with more confidence than knowledge. When I look back at them, I can see how little I understood about the limits of what I understood. I had very little mentorship and plenty of enthusiasm.

I liked explaining things, though. I imagined someone else who had heard a word without knowing what it meant, someone who might hesitate to ask. I was used to being that person. Writing allowed me to offer an explanation without making the reader first admit that they needed one.

Later I discovered that people knew my name from those pieces. It pleased me, and it still does. I had been working things out on a page; elsewhere somebody had found a reason to read. Even now, when I would like to revise half of what my younger self said, I am fond of his eagerness. He didn’t know where any of this was going. He wanted to tell somebody what he had found out.

I need to remember that when I tell the rest of the story. It is easy, once you know where a life has gone, to make every earlier event appear to have been preparing it. The boy in the cold classroom becomes the future professor. The first email account becomes an early sign of a technology career. But I was also the boy who threw away the cone, and that didn’t prepare me for anything in particular. It happened. Much of a life happens before you have a satisfactory account of it.

I never dreamed of working at Microsoft from Balakhu. I hadn’t heard of Microsoft. Harvard was not a destination I had set my heart on. I had to learn that these places existed before they could enter my ambitions. For years, discovering what was possible took up as much effort as pursuing it.

In 2012, I arrived in America with very little money and few people I knew nearby. I later discovered relatives living in states across the country, but at the time I didn’t know some of them existed. They weren’t people whose numbers I had when I needed guidance. There were two people who helped me in ways I will remember for the rest of my life. Whatever they think of me now, I haven’t forgotten. I would not want an account of my independence to erase them.

Still, much of that early period was spent looking for answers myself. Continuing my education was something I wanted to do, but wanting it didn’t tell me whom to contact. There were requirements I had never encountered, procedures other people seemed to understand, decisions that depended on information I didn’t yet know how to find.

Two conversations remain with me.

About two weeks after I arrived, a friend from my higher-secondary-school days in Nepal called. “Ani ta ghanta hando ho?” he asked. Roughly, “So, you’re working by the hour now?”

I took offense. There is nothing shameful about hourly work; the sting was in what I thought he had decided about me. I had barely arrived, and already somebody appeared to have settled the question of what I would be doing. Perhaps he was merely curious. My uncertainty probably made me hear more than he intended. I can acknowledge that now without pretending I wasn’t hurt then.

About six months after arriving, I joined the U.S. Navy. Years later that friend joined the military himself. I have enjoyed the coincidence. I won’t improve the story by pretending to be above it.

The other conversation was with a Nepali friend already living in America. I asked about getting back into school. He described English requirements, IELTS, prerequisites, all the things that would make it difficult. “It’s a long way, bro.” That is how I remember his conclusion.

I wanted to know which university to approach. What should I read? Where was the application? If there was a test, how would I find out whether I needed it? I had asked because I did not know how to begin, and I left with a clearer impression of the obstacles than of anything I could actually do.

I never asked him again.

It is possible he thought he had given sound advice. He may have been describing the system as he understood it. But when you are new to a country, the difference between “this is difficult” and “here is the first thing to do” can occupy weeks of your life. I needed a next step small enough to take. Instead, I went back to looking.

I read, searched, asked other people, made mistakes. Gradually I understood enough to make decisions. I served seven years in the Navy, kept studying, and eventually earned a doctorate in engineering. I moved into cybersecurity and later worked at Microsoft. Government cloud security brought requirements I needed to learn about; privacy and compliance became part of the work. When an internal opportunity opened on Security Copilot, I applied and was selected. My work expanded into responsible AI, sovereign cloud, and data governance. With Microsoft’s support I studied at Harvard. I also began teaching, eventually teaching AI to doctoral students while continuing my professional work.

Those are the facts that usually go into a biography. They are useful facts. They tell an employer or a reader something about the experience I bring. But they leave out the amount of time I spent not knowing which thing would come next, and how often the next thing depended on somebody willing to explain, encourage, or trust me.

I worked hard. That matters to me. So does the fact that work alone does not explain every opportunity I received. Someone had to create the opportunity; someone had to consider me for it. I was prepared for some things because I had studied them and encountered others because I happened to be in the right place. Looking back, I can connect the events. I could not see all those connections while they were happening.

I would feel uncomfortable telling a child from my village that my life proves what they can accomplish if they simply try. I know nothing about what that child may have to carry. My story includes help, institutions, people I met, chances I could have missed. I am proud of it without wanting to turn it into evidence against someone whose hard work has not produced the same result.

What my education did change was my estimate of people I had once regarded as almost another kind of person. I met people who had grown up with money, attended excellent schools, and moved through prestigious institutions from a young age. Some were brilliant. Some were kind. Some were neither especially brilliant nor especially kind, although they had learned to speak with considerable assurance.

They were worried about their children, jobs, health, relationships. They misunderstood things. They could be generous with their time or make a small request seem an imposition. I was relieved to discover how ordinary they were, and a little embarrassed by how much authority I had granted them in my imagination.

Their advantages had brought them into the room. What they did once they were there still had to be judged. I had spent years conflating those two things.

The discovery should have made me immune to the appeal of impressive names. It hasn’t. I still notice them. I am glad to have some attached to my own history. But now I also notice what happens to a conversation after they have been mentioned. The person speaking has not changed. Sometimes the listener changes considerably.

That change in the listener interests me more than a general complaint about people wanting favors. I have asked for help. Friends have done things for me that I could not easily have done myself. If someone I know needs advice, an introduction, or an explanation, I don’t think the request makes the relationship suspect. There would be very little friendship left if we were required to remain useless to each other.

What bothers me is the possibility that usefulness was the qualification for friendship in the first place.

I remember being a young immigrant with a question and little to offer in return. I remember how much an answer would have meant. Now I sometimes feel a warmth that seems to have arrived with the prospect of a future request, and the difference is difficult to overlook. I would rather be asked directly. I may be able to help. I may not. Either way, at least we will know what conversation we are having.

It is much harder when concern for my health appears to be the opening courtesy in a relationship that otherwise depends on my being of service. I cannot know another person’s intention from a message. I can only notice how things develop over time: whether there is interest when I have nothing to offer, whether the conversation survives an inability to help, whether I can be tired and uninteresting without feeling that I have failed to hold up my end.

Illness makes that last question less theoretical. There are days when I have no useful answer about anything, including myself. I can say that I’m recovering. I cannot always provide a reassuring account of how soon I will be better or what the next week will look like. I may want company without wanting questions. I may appreciate the question and still wish it had arrived tomorrow.

I don’t expect everyone to know this. Until you are the person being asked, a question about health seems so obviously considerate that it is hard to imagine its becoming another task. And one question, by itself, often isn’t. Several people asking separately can leave the patient repeating an account of the day instead of resting through it. Nobody has done anything wrong. The result can still be exhausting.

That is part of why I turned off the apps. Another part was the accumulated feeling of being available in too many places, to people whose connection to me ranged from intimate to barely remembered. I had helped create that arrangement. I once enjoyed it.

Twitter gave me arguments to follow and strangers whose thoughts I wanted to read. Instagram accompanied my traveling years. I could share something while it was happening instead of trying to reconstruct it when I got home. Later, at Harvard, WhatsApp helped me keep in touch with classmates from different countries. These tools were useful because our lives had become geographically complicated. I had left a village where visiting required walking and entered a life in which visiting might require crossing an ocean.

The ease of sending a message mattered. It still does. I would not ask someone far away to prove their care by making communication harder than it needs to be. A few words sent after an exhausting day may contain more affection than I can recognize from where I am sitting. I know very little about the hour someone else has had before my name appears on their phone.

But ease can also make it possible for me to remain aware of a person without having much of a relationship with them. I see the photograph, recognize the child who has grown older, learn about a new job. The information is recent. The last conversation may be years old.

This is an unsettling combination. I know enough to feel that we are in touch and too little to know what being in touch would reveal. I can congratulate someone on the development they announced without having heard about the months that preceded it. They can do the same for me. We are familiar with each other’s news and increasingly unfamiliar with each other.

I have friendships like this. Some of you reading are in them with me. It would be absurd to blame the phone for the fact that neither of us has called. But the occasional update has made our silence less noticeable. A person can remain visibly present in my life while I have no idea how they are spending the difficult parts of it.

Then surgery appears, and there is suddenly a reason to ask.

I am glad people asked. I am also aware that I had not given them much else to ask about for a long time. I shared work, writing, professional developments. I kept most of the pain private. If the picture people have of me is disproportionately professional, I supplied a good deal of the material. I cannot expect a friendship to know what I have consistently left out of it.

That does not settle my uneasiness about attention and status. It makes the matter less convenient to explain. People can be drawn to what I have accomplished because it is interesting, because they are proud of me, because it is the part of my life I have made available, or because they want something. I cannot read all these motives from the same friendly sentence.

Nor do I want to spend recovery trying.

The people I keep thinking about are the ones who helped before there was any obvious advantage in doing so. I remember them with a clarity that my memory rarely gives to professional praise. They could not have known what I would go on to do. They knew what I needed then, and did something about it. It is hard to explain how long that kind of help stays with you, or how it changes your understanding of what another person’s attention can be worth.

I don’t want to confuse that with a belief that the village was better in every way. I left Balakhu because there were things elsewhere I wanted to discover. I have spent my adult life using and working on technologies that reduce the difficulties I grew up with. I would have welcomed a telephone long before sixth grade. My mother and I could have used a road.

In the village where I grew up, I watched people leave their work to visit a sick neighbor. They walked over, sat with the family, and stayed a while. As a boy, I hardly noticed what those visits required. This week, lying in a hospital bed thousands of miles away, I kept thinking about them.

What I remember about those visits is that the sick person did not have to provide anything especially interesting in return. People sat with the family. They asked and listened. The visit could consist of very little happening. There was no accomplishment to celebrate or useful information to carry away. Someone was there because somebody else was ill.

Perhaps that is why the memory returns so insistently now. I would like to be cared about on days when I have nothing to contribute. I suspect most people would. Yet so much of the life I have built rewards me for having something to contribute that I am still learning how to be comfortable without it.

I enjoy my work. This tends to get lost when people advise me to do less of it.

Technology has interested me since I was trying to understand that first email account. I like having a problem to work through, something I don’t quite understand, a reason to read beyond what I already know. Teaching gives me another way into the same subjects. A question that seems settled at work can become difficult again when I have to explain why the decision made sense. I enjoy that difficulty, even on days when I could have used a simpler question.

There are meetings I would happily miss, administrative tasks I put off, weeks when I have agreed to too much. But I chose much of this. I wanted to teach alongside my other work. I pursued opportunities because I was interested. When someone tells me I should make more time for things I enjoy, I sometimes want to explain what I have actually been doing with the time.

My mornings usually start with NPR and The New York Times. Then I listen to podcasts: politics, economics, international affairs, cybersecurity, whatever catches my attention. Working from home allows me to have something playing during parts of the day. One discussion sends me to another. I can spend hours this way and remain convinced there is one more thing worth hearing.

I even listen in the shower. At this point I ought to know enough about the world to get through washing without an update. Apparently I don’t.

Some of this curiosity has helped me professionally. I noticed subjects becoming important and wanted to learn more about them. Some of it has simply been enjoyable. I don’t need every article I read to justify itself by becoming useful at work. But I have become rather good at making interest into a reason to stay occupied, and less good at noticing when I have had enough for one day.

For years, the person doing all this reading and working was also in pain. Those years have included some of the best opportunities of my life and some of the worst days for my health. I could be excited about a project while having a miserable week physically. A professional update could be entirely true and tell you almost nothing about how I felt while writing it.

I wanted to keep it that way. I didn’t want every conversation to begin with an inquiry about my health. I had work I liked, students to teach, things I wanted to finish. Much of it could be done from a computer, which was fortunate. I arranged appointments around other commitments and changed plans when I had to. A difficult day became something to work out how to manage.

After a while, I no longer noticed all the managing. It was part of getting through the week. If I could do a task, I tended to do it. There was always something small enough to seem reasonable, and several reasonable tasks could add up to a day in which I had done rather more than I should have.

The enjoyment complicated this. I wasn’t enduring every hour of work until I could return to a life I preferred. Sometimes the work was what I preferred, especially to spending more time thinking about the pain. I wanted the interesting parts of my life to continue. They did, and I am glad. I would have liked to experience them in a healthier body.

Before it, coughing and sneezing could hurt. This week I caught myself expecting the pain as a cough approached, already apprehensive about what was going to happen. Then I coughed, and it didn’t hurt in the way I expected.

I had prepared for a sensation that didn’t arrive.

For the moment, that is one of the things I am happiest about. My leg pain is much better. There is discomfort around the incision, and I am being careful. Five days is early. I can feel encouraged without knowing how the rest of recovery will go.

I am taking short walks along the sidewalk in my neighborhood. How fast should I walk? How much is enough? These are the questions occupying me now. I am trying to follow the instructions I was given, rest when I need to, and avoid interpreting every improvement as permission to do more. It is an unfamiliar kind of day for someone accustomed to filling spare time before it has a chance to remain spare.

I don’t believe the pain has made me better at my work. I would rather have done the same work without it. Nor do I feel obliged to extract an uplifting lesson from surgery. Mostly I want to recover. But resting has left me with memories and questions I would ordinarily put aside until I had finished something more urgent. Now there is less reason to put them aside, and more time to notice that some have been waiting for years.

Among them is the question of why I have become so comfortable alone.

There were years when I didn’t have much choice about finding my own answers. I learned to look things up, make decisions, get on with what needed doing. Later, when there were more people I could ask, asking did not immediately become natural. I was used to managing. Explaining a problem to somebody else could feel like an additional piece of work, especially if I was uncertain whether they could help.

That habit has served me well. It can also make other people unnecessary in situations where they might have been glad to be included. I don’t mean only help with a difficult problem. Sometimes a friend is someone you tell before you know what you think, before there is a decision to report. I have become rather practiced at appearing afterward, with the problem handled and a brief account of how things went.

Then I wonder why people know the outcomes better than they know me.

I can see my part in that. Seeing it doesn’t mean I suddenly want more company every day. I like my home. I like deciding what to read and how to spend an afternoon. There are weekends when I don’t want to go anywhere, and I am not unhappy during them. I am enjoying a degree of choice I worked hard to obtain.

Some people find this difficult to believe. “What’s the point of making money if you don’t know how to have fun?” I have heard versions of that question often enough.

Those who remember my Travel in Twenties blog or Instagram may be able to answer it on my behalf. I traveled extensively. I met people, explored places, shared photographs, and had a wonderful time. There were even news articles about some of those adventures. I wanted to see things for myself, and I did. I am glad I have those years to remember.

I don’t need to disown that younger man to enjoy being home now. He wanted different things because he had done fewer things. I still like traveling. I can look forward to another trip without feeling that the days between trips are waiting to become worthwhile.

Today I live comfortably. Thank God, I have financial security and choices that were once unavailable to me. I know how fortunate that makes me. Money has spared me worries I remember having; I have no interest in pretending it doesn’t matter. One of the things it has made possible, though, is a fairly ordinary day in a place where I am content. I would like to be allowed to enjoy that without having to make it look more exciting.

For a long time, leaving seemed to be what a promising life required. Leave the village, reach the city, go abroad, find the next opportunity. Each departure gave me something I could not have had by staying exactly where I was. It is understandable that I learned to regard movement as evidence that things were going well.

Now I can afford more movement and don’t always want it. That has taken some getting used to. I am still ambitious. I want to teach, write, work on questions I find important. I just don’t want the fact that I once had fewer choices to commit me to a lifetime of demonstrating how many I have acquired.

Of course, a quiet Sunday for me may be another unanswered call for somebody else.

I know friends who have done more than I have to keep us in touch. They remember, ask, try again. I read a message while occupied, decide to answer later, and let the interval grow until replying seems to require an explanation. Then I postpone the explanation. This is an embarrassingly elaborate way to avoid writing a few sentences to someone I would be pleased to hear from.

I have good reasons for needing quiet right now. They don’t account for every silence over the years. Sometimes I have been careless. Sometimes I have assumed that people who know me will understand, without considering how often I have asked them to do the understanding.

I don’t want a vocabulary in which every withdrawal becomes wise simply because I call it a boundary. There are afternoons I should keep for myself and conversations I have avoided because another person’s life would interrupt what I wanted to do. Friendship is full of such interruptions. I knew that as a child watching adults leave their work to visit someone, though I would not have thought to call it a principle.

The visitors didn’t wait until there was nothing else to do. There would always be something else. At some point they went.

I have spent much of this essay wondering about the quality of attention other people give me. It would be convenient to stop there. But I am also the person with unfinished work and a phone I would sometimes rather ignore. I want to be cared about without always having the patience to care well in return. Admitting that is uncomfortable. It seems more useful than another explanation of how busy I have been.

I am not promising to answer every message, and certainly not while recovering. I would like to become less likely to let a good friendship disappear into the assumption that we will catch up eventually. There are people I miss whom I haven’t told I miss. They cannot be expected to know it from my silence.

Writing this has also made me think about my mother on the journey to that first bus. For years I told the story from my seat by the window: the diesel, the vendors, the frightening vehicle I couldn’t walk in front of. My mother was present because she was the reason I had gone. I gave very little thought to her experience of the same trip.

She had work to attend to after the walk and the overnight stop. I had a bus to be excited about. Looking back now, I understand how much could happen in the same day that a child would never consider asking about. My memory is vivid, but its attention is very narrow. It follows me all the way to the window and stays there.

I would like to get better at looking beyond it.

There are stories I haven’t told, people whose help deserves more space than I have given it, mistakes I can write about now without needing to make myself right. I miss personal writing.
